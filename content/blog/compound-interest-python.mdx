Compound interest is growth on top of growth. It is the reason time matters so
much in investing, and it is easy to see once you write it as code.

*The rates below are illustrative only. They are not a forecast or a promise of
any return.*

## The idea

If you earn a return and leave it invested, next year you earn a return on the
original amount **and** on last year's gain. The formula for a one-time deposit
is:

```
future value = principal × (1 + rate) ^ years
```

## One deposit, three time horizons

```python
principal = 1000
rate = 0.07  # an illustrative 7% a year

for years in (10, 20, 30):
    value = principal * (1 + rate) ** years
    print(years, round(value, 2))
```

This prints:

```
10 1967.15
20 3869.68
30 7612.26
```

Notice the pattern. The second ten years added about $1,900 and the third ten
added about $3,700. The growth speeds up because each year's gain is larger than
the last.

## The Rule of 72

A quick mental shortcut: divide 72 by the annual rate (as a whole number) to
estimate how many years it takes to double. At 7%, 72 ÷ 7 is about 10.3 years,
which matches the table above, where $1,000 became about $1,967 in 10 years. It
is an approximation, not an exact law.

## Adding money every month

Most people invest regularly, not once. Here is $100 a month, compounded monthly:

```python
monthly = 100
rate = 0.07
months = 30 * 12
r = rate / 12

value = monthly * (((1 + r) ** months - 1) / r)
print(round(value))        # 121997
print(monthly * months)    # 36000 put in
```

Over 30 years you contribute $36,000, and in this illustration it grows to about
$122,000. Most of that final number is growth, not deposits, and most of the
growth comes in the later years.

## Why starting early matters

Because growth builds on itself, the same dollar invested sooner has more years to
compound. Waiting a decade to start is not made up by saving a little more later;
it costs you the years where compounding does the most work.

## The warning label

Real investments do not earn the same return every year. They go up and down,
and sometimes lose money. A smooth 7% line is a teaching tool. Fees also reduce
growth: at 6% instead of 7%, that first $1,000 grows to about $5,743 in 30 years
instead of $7,612.
