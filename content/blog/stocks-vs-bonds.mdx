Stocks and bonds are the two building blocks of most portfolios. They look
similar on a brokerage screen, but they are fundamentally different promises.

*This article is education, not investment advice.*

## Stocks are ownership

When you buy a stock, you own part of a company. You do well if the company
grows and its share price rises, and you may also receive **dividends**, a share
of profits some companies pay out. There is no promised return. If the company
struggles, the stock can fall a lot, and in the worst case become worthless.

## Bonds are loans

When you buy a bond, you lend money to a government or company. In return they
promise to pay you **interest** (called the coupon) on a schedule and to give back
your original amount (the **face value**) on a set date called maturity.

A $1,000 bond with a 5% coupon pays $50 a year in interest, then returns the
$1,000 at maturity, as long as the borrower can pay.

## How the risks differ

- **Stocks:** price swings, and the company may do badly. Higher potential
  growth, higher potential loss.
- **Bonds:** the borrower may fail to pay (**credit risk**), and the bond's market
  price changes when interest rates change (**interest-rate risk**). Bonds are
  usually less volatile than stocks, but they are not risk-free.

## Why bond prices move when rates change

This surprises most beginners. Suppose you own a bond paying 5%, and new bonds
now pay 6%. Nobody will pay full price for your 5% bond when they can get 6%
elsewhere, so its market price falls. If new bonds pay 4%, your 5% bond becomes
more attractive and its price rises.

You can check this with a small calculation. Take a $1,000 bond with a 5% coupon
and ten years left, and discount each payment at the going rate:

```python
def bond_price(face, coupon_rate, market_rate, years):
    coupon = face * coupon_rate
    pv_coupons = sum(coupon / (1 + market_rate) ** t for t in range(1, years + 1))
    pv_face = face / (1 + market_rate) ** years
    return pv_coupons + pv_face

print(round(bond_price(1000, 0.05, 0.06, 10), 2))  # 926.4
print(round(bond_price(1000, 0.05, 0.04, 10), 2))  # 1081.11
```

When the market rate rises from 5% to 6%, the bond is worth about $926. When it
falls to 4%, it is worth about $1,081. Same bond, different price.

## Why people hold both

Stocks and bonds often react differently to the same events, so holding both can
make a portfolio's ups and downs smoother. How much of each is a personal choice
that depends on your goals and how long you can wait, which is why advice from a
qualified adult matters for real money decisions.
