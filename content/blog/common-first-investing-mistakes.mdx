Most beginner losses come from a short list of avoidable mistakes. This list is
for learning, not advice about what to buy.

## 1. Chasing what just went up

A stock that rose fast makes the news, and buying it feels safe because
everyone is talking about it. But by then the price already reflects the good
news. Buying because something is popular, or because you fear missing out, is
not a plan.

## 2. Putting everything in one stock

One company can fail, however good it looks. Spreading money across many
investments, as a broad index fund does, means one failure cannot sink you. See
[what an ETF is](/blog/what-is-an-etf).

## 3. Investing money you may need soon

Prices can fall and stay down for a long time. Money for next year's bills,
tuition, or an emergency should not be exposed to market swings. Investing is for
money you can leave alone for years.

## 4. Ignoring fees

A 1% yearly fee sounds small. Over decades it can take a large share of your
growth because it compounds against you. Always find the expense ratio and any
trading costs before you buy. See the numbers in the
[compound interest example](/blog/compound-interest-python).

## 5. Trading too often

Frequent buying and selling adds costs and tends to turn small decisions into
emotional ones. Most long-term investors do better by deciding a plan and mostly
sticking with it.

## 6. Using borrowed money or complicated products

Borrowing to invest (leverage), and instruments like options, can magnify losses
as well as gains, and you can lose more than you put in. Understanding them first
is worth the time. The [options lessons](/lessons) are a safe place to practice
because nothing real is at stake.

## 7. Falling for scams

Warning signs include:

- A **guaranteed** high return. No honest investment can guarantee one.
- Pressure to act **right now**.
- A stranger or a social media account offering inside tips or "signals."
- Being asked to pay in gift cards, crypto transfers, or by wire to a person.
- Anything you do not understand but are told is "can't lose."

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's [investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov)
has plain-language warnings and a way to check whether a person or firm is
registered. If something feels off, stop and talk to a trusted adult.

## A simple habit

Before putting in money, write down why you are buying, how long you plan to hold,
and what would make you sell. If you cannot write it, you are guessing.
