Picking individual stocks means betting on specific companies. An
**exchange-traded fund**, or ETF, lets you hold many investments at once through
a single purchase. It is one of the most common ways ordinary investors get
broad exposure to the market.

*This article is education, not investment advice. It does not recommend any
fund.*

## The basic idea

An ETF is a fund that owns a collection of assets, such as stocks or bonds, and
sells shares of itself. Those shares trade on an exchange throughout the day,
just like a stock. When you buy one share of an ETF, you own a tiny slice of
everything in the basket.

## Index ETFs

Many ETFs are built to follow an **index**, a published list of securities that
represents part of the market. An S&P 500 index ETF, for example, holds roughly
the 500 large U.S. companies in that index, in proportions that mirror the
index. It does not try to beat the market. It tries to match it.

## Why people use them

- **Diversification.** One purchase spreads your money across many companies, so
  one company doing badly matters much less.
- **Low cost.** Many index ETFs charge very little to run.
- **Simplicity.** You do not have to research hundreds of companies.

## The expense ratio

Every fund charges a yearly fee, shown as a percentage of the money invested
called the **expense ratio**. On $1,000 invested:

- A 0.05% expense ratio costs $0.50 a year.
- A 1.00% expense ratio costs $10 a year.

That gap looks small, but it repeats every year and compounds. Over decades, a
higher fee can take a meaningful share of what you would otherwise have kept.

## What ETFs do not do

Diversification reduces the risk of one company failing. It does **not** remove
market risk. If the whole market falls, a fund that tracks it falls too. An ETF
can lose value, and nothing guarantees a return. Some ETFs are also narrow,
focused on one industry, country, or strategy, and can be much riskier than a
broad index fund. Read what a fund actually holds before judging it.

## ETF versus mutual fund

Both pool money from many investors. The practical difference is that ETFs trade
on an exchange all day at a changing price, while traditional mutual funds are
priced once at the end of the trading day.
