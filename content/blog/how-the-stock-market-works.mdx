The stock market is not a single place and it is not a casino. It is a set of
organized marketplaces where people agree on a price to buy and sell small
pieces of companies. Understanding how that agreement happens is the first
step to understanding almost everything else in investing.

*This article is education, not investment advice. It does not recommend any
stock, fund, or strategy.*

## What a share is

A **share** (or stock) is a unit of ownership in a company. If a company has
one million shares and you own one thousand of them, you own one-tenth of one
percent of the company. Companies sell shares to raise money, and the people who
buy them hope the company will grow and be worth more later.

## What an exchange does

An **exchange**, such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq, is a marketplace
that matches buyers and sellers. It does not decide what a stock is worth. It
makes sure that when one person wants to sell and another wants to buy at a
price they both accept, the trade happens in an orderly, recorded way.

In the United States, regular trading hours are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern
time on business days.

## Bid, ask, and spread

At any moment there are two prices for a stock:

- The **bid** is the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.
- The **ask** is the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.

The gap between them is the **spread**. If the bid is $50.00 and the ask is
$50.05, the spread is five cents. A buyer who needs the stock right now pays the
ask; a seller who needs to sell right now receives the bid. The spread is a real
cost of trading, and it is usually tiny for large, popular companies and larger
for small, rarely traded ones.

## Why prices move

Prices move because the balance of buyers and sellers changes. News about a
company, the economy, interest rates, or just a change in mood can make more
people want to buy than sell, which pushes the price up, or the reverse. No one
person sets the price. It is the running result of thousands of decisions.

## Two kinds of orders

- A **market order** says "buy or sell now at the best available price." It is
  fast, but you may get a slightly different price than the one you last saw.
- A **limit order** says "buy or sell only at this price or better." It protects
  you on price, but it might never fill if the market does not reach your price.

## What happens after you click buy

Your broker sends the order to a market, it is matched, and the trade is
recorded. In the U.S., most stock trades have settled one business day after the
trade since May 2024, a convention called T+1, which is when ownership and cash
officially change hands.

## The short version

Exchanges match buyers and sellers. The bid and ask show where they currently
disagree, the spread is the cost of that disagreement, and the price you see is
simply where the most recent trade happened. Everything else in investing is
built on top of that.
